How to use this guide

References take the form III.286: Act III, line 286, where each line of dialogue counts as one line, however long. A stage direction is referenced to the line it follows, for example III.381 s.d.. Lines are counted from the start of each act in R. Farquharson Sharp’s translation. Act I has 485 lines, Act II 426 and Act III 381.

Built for Paper 2. The issue arcs trace each concern through the three acts, and Dig deeper sets out the critical readings to test against the play.

The examination

Course and paperIB Diploma English A: Language and Literature, Paper 2: the comparative essay on two literary works studied in the course. Paper 2 is not a set-text exam: the questions are general, they do not name any work, and there are no extracts.
Format1 hour 45 minutes at both SL and HL. Answer one of four general questions, comparing two of the literary works you have studied. 25 marks.
CriteriaA Knowledge, understanding and interpretation (5). B Analysis and evaluation (10), in two strands: B1 how meaning is shaped by language, technique, style and broader authorial choices (5); B2 the similarities and differences between the two works (5). C Focus and organisation (5). D Language (5).
What this means for revisionYou will not know the question in advance, so you cannot prepare a single essay. You prepare a small number of moments from each work that you know in detail, grouped by issue, and you rehearse how each work handles those issues differently. That is what the issue arcs, the event bank and the quote bank in this guide are for. Comparison must be structural: every paragraph needs both works, and criterion B2 assesses it directly.
iSWIM for Paper 2. Use the five guiding questions on every prompt.
iWhat is the issue of significance? The concern beneath the prompt’s topic word.
SWhy does it matter? The human, social or philosophical stakes.
WWhat does each author want you to understand about it?
IHow does each author get you to understand it? Structure, form, characterisation, voice, staging.
MWhat does each work position its audience towards, and what does the comparison reveal? The essay-level M comes from the tension between M(A) and M(B).

Write each paragraph with PEACE ACT: Point, Evidence, Analysis, Comparison, Extension, Audience, Context, Tie-back. Characters do not exist: reach through the characters to the choices the writer makes with them.

iSWIM tells you how to plan. Thinking with iSWIM tells you how to think. Your text-type guide tells you what to notice and what to call it.

Context

Facts about the writer and period are attributed to a named source. Facts about the work come from the work.

Composition and first performance

Ibsen wrote the play in 1879 while living in Italy and Germany; it was first performed at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen in December 1879.

Source: LitCharts; Barron’s Book Notes

Laura Kieler

The plot draws on the case of Ibsen’s friend Laura Kieler, who borrowed money in secret to pay for her husband’s recovery and was later committed to an asylum when the debt was discovered.

Source: LitCharts

The alternative ending

For an early German production, Ibsen wrote an alternative ending in which Nora, seeing her children, cannot leave. He called it a barbaric outrage on the play and wrote it only to prevent someone else from rewriting the ending.

Source: LitCharts; Ibsen’s letters

Ibsen and the women’s movement

In 1898, at a banquet of the Norwegian Women’s Rights League, Ibsen said he had not consciously worked for the women’s cause and that his task had been the description of humanity.

Source: Ibsen, speech of 1898

Event bank

14 numbered events in order. Read one act for close revision, or E1 to E14 straight through as the arc of the whole work.

Act I

E1 Christmas shopping and macaroons I.1–10

Nora comes home with parcels and a Christmas tree, tips the porter generously and secretly eats macaroons I.3. Helmer calls her his lark and squirrel I.4 and teases her about spending I.10.

Why it matters. The opening shows the marriage as a performance of pet names and small deceptions, with money at the centre.

Dig deeper. Why does the play open with a lie about macaroons? What does the smallness of the lie prepare us for?

E2 Nora’s secret I.85–173

Nora’s old friend Christine Linde arrives, widowed and poor I.85 s.d.. Nora reveals that the year in Italy that saved Helmer’s life I.125 was paid for with money she raised herself I.173, and that she has been repaying it secretly, partly by copying I.207.

Why it matters. The doll of the first scene is revealed as the one who acted, in secret, to save her husband.

Dig deeper. Nora is proud of the loan. Why does Ibsen let the audience see her pride before it sees the danger?

E3 Krogstad’s threat I.218–399

Krogstad, a clerk at Helmer’s bank, is about to be dismissed. He tells Nora that the bond she signed bears her father’s signature dated three days after his death I.399: she forged it.

Why it matters. The secret becomes a crime, and the plot’s machinery starts to turn.

E4 Helmer on lies and mothers I.464–476

Helmer explains why Krogstad is morally lost I.464: lying poisons a home I.474, and almost everyone who goes to the bad has had a deceitful mother I.476.

Why it matters. Helmer condemns his wife without knowing it; the act ends with Nora afraid of corrupting her children.

Act II

E5 The costume and the tarantella II.30–139

Nora is to dance the tarantella at the fancy-dress ball II.30, and pleads with Helmer to keep Krogstad on. Helmer refuses, partly because Krogstad is too familiar with him II.119, and sends the dismissal letter.

Why it matters. Nora’s influence over Helmer is shown to be limited to play.

E6 Doctor Rank II.38–214

Rank, dying of an inherited disease II.38, flirts with Nora over silk stockings II.188 and declares that he loves her II.214. She refuses to ask him for the money.

Why it matters. Nora could buy her way out through a man’s love and chooses not to.

Dig deeper. Why does Nora stop herself from asking Rank for help once he declares his love?

E7 The letter in the box II.307–323

Krogstad reveals his ambition II.307 and drops a letter revealing everything into Helmer’s locked letter-box II.323.

Why it matters. A single prop now holds the plot: everyone on stage can see the letter, and only Helmer has the key.

E8 The wonderful thing II.329–341

Nora confesses the forgery to Mrs Linde II.329 and says she expects “a wonderful thing” II.341: that Helmer will take the guilt on himself. She dances the tarantella wildly to delay him.

Why it matters. Nora’s hope sets the test Helmer will fail in Act III.

Act III

E9 Mrs Linde and Krogstad III.9–77

Mrs Linde, who once left Krogstad to marry for money, offers to marry him now III.9. She tells him not to recall his letter III.75: Helmer must know everything III.77.

Why it matters. A second couple chooses honesty and partnership, as a counterweight to the Helmers.

Dig deeper. Why does Mrs Linde, who could stop the letter, decide that Helmer must read it?

E10 Helmer reads the letter III.236–238

Helmer reads Krogstad’s letter and turns on Nora: she is a hypocrite, a liar and a criminal III.236, and has destroyed his happiness III.238.

Why it matters. The wonderful thing does not happen: Helmer thinks only of himself.

E11 The second letter and the “forgiveness” III.251–253

A second letter returns the bond. Helmer is saved, and at once forgives Nora III.253.

Why it matters. His forgiveness depends on his safety, which Nora now sees clearly.

E12 The first serious conversation III.262–286

Nora sits Helmer down III.262 and points out that in eight years they have never had a serious conversation III.270. She has been his doll-wife as she was her father’s doll-child III.286.

Why it matters. The play changes form: from intrigue to discussion.

E13 Duties to herself III.314–316

Against Helmer’s “most sacred duties” III.309, Nora sets duties to herself III.314 and the claim to be a reasonable human being first III.316.

Why it matters. The play’s central claim, made in the plainest language.

E14 The door III.376–381

Nora leaves her keys and her ring III.366, says the most wonderful thing would be a real marriage III.380, and goes. Helmer is left with a flash of hope as a door shuts below III.381 s.d..

Why it matters. The ending is a sound, not a resolution: the audience does not see what Nora becomes.

Dig deeper. Ibsen wrote an alternative ending for a German production in which Nora stays for the children. Why might he have called it a barbaric outrage, and what would the play lose?

Ibsen’s methods

AO3: how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form.

The well-made play, then the discussion

Acts I and II use the machinery of the well-made play: a secret, a threatening letter, a deadline. In Act III Nora sits down III.262 and the play becomes a discussion, which Shaw saw as Ibsen’s technical innovation.

Symbolic props

The macaroons I.3, the Christmas tree, the letter-box II.323, the costume and the door III.381 s.d..

Dramatic irony

Helmer’s speech on deceitful mothers I.476 is delivered to the woman who has done what he describes.

Parallel couples

Mrs Linde and Krogstad choose honesty III.9 while the Helmers’ marriage collapses.

Naturalistic dialogue

Ordinary language carries the climax: “Duties to myself.” III.314.

Dig deeper

The surface overview is in the event bank and issue arcs. These sections go further, into Ibsen’s own statements, the play’s form, its economics, its use of performance, and the debate over its ending. Every claim about the play is referenced; critical readings are offered for evaluation.

Ibsen’s own words

On the surface. The play is a feminist manifesto.

In his notes for the play (1878) Ibsen wrote that there are two kinds of spiritual law and two kinds of conscience, one in man and another in woman, and that a woman cannot be herself in a society made by men. The plot puts those two consciences in conflict: Nora broke the law to save a life I.173; Helmer judges by appearances III.236.

Twenty years later he told the Norwegian Women’s Rights League that he had not consciously worked for women’s rights; his task had been the description of humanity. It could be argued that Nora’s claim to be “a reasonable human being” III.316 supports that reading: she claims humanity before womanhood.

Questions to explore
  • Can a play be feminist if its author denied writing for feminism?
  • Does it matter what Ibsen intended?

From well-made play to discussion

On the surface. A melodrama of secrets and letters.

Shaw argued in The Quintessence of Ibsenism that Ibsen’s innovation was the discussion: at the climax, instead of a revelation followed by reconciliation, the characters sit down and argue. Nora draws attention to it herself III.270.

The well-made plot (secret, letter, deadline) is used and then abandoned, as if the play outgrows its own form.

Questions to explore
  • Why might a debate be more dramatic than a revelation?

Money and the marriage market

On the surface. A domestic drama.

Almost every relationship in the play is financial: Helmer’s promotion, Nora’s loan, Krogstad’s job, Mrs Linde’s first marriage for money. A Marxist or economic reading could argue that Nora’s description of her marriage III.282 describes property, not love.

Questions to explore
  • Is Nora freed by refusing Helmer’s money, or made poor?

Performance and the self

On the surface. Nora pretends.

It could be argued, following critics such as Toril Moi, that the play is about theatricality: Nora performs the role Helmer wants, dances the tarantella as a performance of panic II.30, and only stops performing when she changes out of her costume and sits down to talk.

Questions to explore
  • Is the Nora of Act III more real, or another role?

The ending and its critics

On the surface. Nora leaves.

The door was shocking in 1879; Ibsen had to write an alternative ending for one German production. Critics have debated whether Nora’s departure is believable, whether she abandons her children, and whether Helmer’s last flash of hope III.381 leaves any possibility of change.

Questions to explore
  • What happens to Nora next? Why does Ibsen refuse to tell us?
  • Would the play be stronger or weaker if Nora stayed?
